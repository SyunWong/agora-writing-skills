# Work message polish

June writes to overseas colleagues in English, usually starting from Chinese notes or from her own English draft. The job is a message she can paste and send. It should sound like her, and it should be short.

The rules are layered and apply in this order. A later layer never overrides an earlier one.

## Layer 1: which job is this

Decide before touching the text.

| She gives you | Job | What you may change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Her own English draft, asks "is this right" or "check this" | Grammar check | Only what is wrong or ambiguous |
| Her own English draft, asks to polish or improve | Light edit | Wrong, ambiguous, or longer than it needs to be |
| Chinese notes or bullets | Write it in English | Everything, inside the rules below |
| A message she received plus what she wants to say back | Write the reply | Everything, inside the rules below |

If it isn't clear which channel the message goes to, or who reads it, ask once. A ClickUp comment and a Slack DM come out differently.

## Layer 2: facts

Get these right before style. Each one has gone wrong in a real draft.

- **Names and tools.** Check the spelling of every person and tool against what she wrote earlier in the chat: Hermes, Claudia, Omja, ClickUp, Medium, Lark. Fix typos in her draft without comment.
- **What the thing is.** Say exactly what is being referred to. "Our promo video that played at the RTE conference" and "a video of the RTE conference" are different things. "Make the screenshots larger" is a size request, and "higher resolution" is a different one. "Format the commands as code blocks" is about formatting and says nothing about the code being wrong.
- **Which account, which place.** "Agora's official X and YouTube accounts", never just "X". "The website blog", never just "the blog", when Medium is also in play.
- **Dates and owners.** If a date or an owner changed, state the new one plainly. Don't make the reader work out the difference.
- **Links.** If the reader needs a page to act (the Medium post, the ClickUp task, the draft), the link goes in the message. If she didn't give one, leave a visible `[link]` and say so.

Never add a fact, a deadline or a reason she didn't give. If the message needs one to make sense, ask.

## Layer 3: the ask

- One message, one ask where possible. If there are three asks, number them or give each its own line.
- When the ask costs the reader time or changes how they work, phrase it as a question. "Could you review website posts before Medium ones?" lands better than "Please prioritize website posts."
- Put the ask where it can't be missed: first line in a DM, last line after two lines of context in a channel post.
- Give an easy way to say no when the ask is a favor: "Totally fine if you'd rather not."
- A reminder is shorter than the original request. Mention, link, one line.

## Layer 4: what stays inside the team

Messages to marketing, design or external partners leave these out:

- Internal uncertainty and friction: QA workload, why something slipped, who was late.
- Anything that assigns blame, including to the reader. If the reader has an old date because the update hadn't gone out yet, the correction is "a few things shifted on our side", followed by the new date.
- Internal test numbers and competitor claims that haven't been cleared for publication.

Status goes in neutral terms: what is done, what is next, when.

## Layer 5: voice

Peer to peer, casual, plain.

- No warm-up. Cut "Hey!", "Hope you're doing well", "Quick question", "Just wanted to". Start with the name or the point. "Hey Hermes," is fine for someone she talks to daily.
- No closer. Cut "Thanks so much in advance!", "Let me know if you have any questions", "Looking forward to hearing from you". A bare "Thanks!" is fine after a favor.
- Plain verbs over eager ones: "happy to help" over "would love to", "we'll let you know" over "we'll keep you posted", "sync" over "align".
- Keep her words when they work. If she wrote "blogs", don't change it to "blog posts". If she wrote in lowercase, it stays lowercase.
- Match the relationship. Someone she works with every week gets a looser message than someone she's writing to for the first time. Ask if you can't tell.
- One emoji at most, and only if she used one or the message is a light ask to someone she knows well.
- Then run the `humanizer` skill's shared layer and English word lists. No em dashes.

## Layer 6: length

Default lengths. Go over only if the facts need it.

| Channel | Length |
| --- | --- |
| Slack DM | 1 to 3 sentences |
| Slack channel or group update | Up to about 80 words. Labeled lines (RD status, GTM status) are fine for a multi-part sync |
| ClickUp comment | @mention, one line, link |
| X reply as herself | One or two short sentences. Skip steps the reader already knows |
| Email | A short paragraph plus the ask |

If her draft is already this short, don't lengthen it.

## What to deliver

1. The message, ready to paste, in a code block or on its own lines. Nothing before it.
2. For a grammar check or light edit, a short list under it in two groups:
   - **Had to change:** grammar, spelling, ambiguity. One line each.
   - **Optional:** style suggestions, two at most, each marked as her call. Leave this group out when there is nothing worth raising.
3. For a ClickUp comment, one line on whether a status change or reassignment would do more than the comment.

Reply to her in Chinese unless she writes in English. Don't explain a change she didn't ask about, and don't offer three variants unless she asks for options or the tone is in doubt. When she pushes back on a word, change that word and leave the rest alone.

## Final check

- [ ] Every name and tool spelled right
- [ ] The thing referred to can't be read two ways
- [ ] The ask is one sentence and easy to find
- [ ] Nothing internal leaked, nobody blamed
- [ ] No opener, no closer, no em dash
- [ ] Not longer than her draft unless a fact was missing
- [ ] "Had to change" and "Optional" are not mixed together

## How this goes wrong

Taken from real drafts.

- **Presenting style changes as corrections.** "blog posts" for "blogs", an added "Hey!", an added sign-off. She asked which edits were needed and these weren't.
- **Too formal for the relationship.** "Could you take a look at" to someone she chats with daily. She had to say they already work together.
- **"Would love to" in a casual DM.** The tone didn't match the question.
- **"Quick question" as an opener.** She rejected it. Go straight to the question.
- **A demand where a question belonged.** A review-priority request written as a statement read as pushy until it became a question.
- **Describing the wrong object.** A promo video shown at a conference written up as conference footage.
- **Explaining too much.** She has asked for shorter answers with less commentary more than once.
- **Changing more than the word she flagged.** When she corrects one phrase, the rest of the message was already approved.
